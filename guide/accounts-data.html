The accounts data: mostly balance sheets

Summary

Almost every UK company must file annual accounts, and Companies House publishes the electronically filed ones as a free bulk archive. But what a company files depends on its size, and most UK companies are small or micro. Small and micro companies may file “filleted” accounts — the balance sheet and its notes only, with no profit-and-loss account. The practical result is that, for most companies, the public data has no turnover and no profit figure at all: it is, in effect, a balance-sheet dataset. Accounts are tagged in iXBRL, a format that is both human-readable in a browser and machine-readable, so the figures can be extracted at scale.

Two changes matter for anyone using this data. Size thresholds rose on 6 April 2025 (their first change since 2013), moving some companies into lighter-disclosure bands. And from April 2028, reforms will require small companies to file a profit-and-loss account — but that will not fill in the past, so anyone modelling history still works with balance-sheet-only data.

Details

Who files what

Under the Companies Act 2006, essentially every registered company must file annual accounts (private companies have nine months after the year-end). A company’s size band is set by meeting two of three tests — turnover, balance-sheet total, and average employees — over two consecutive years. From 6 April 2025 the monetary thresholds rose by roughly half:

Band Turnover Balance sheet Employees
Micro-entity ≤ £1m ≤ £500k ≤ 10
Small ≤ £15m ≤ £7.5m ≤ 50
Medium ≤ £54m ≤ £27m ≤ 250

Source: deck slides 11–12 and the accounts explainer (docs/source-material/accounts-explainer.html); thresholds via Lexology summary of the 6 April 2025 change. [Checked] (official change date; figures are the published thresholds).

Filleted accounts are the defining feature. Under section 444 of the Act, small companies and micro-entities prepare full accounts for their shareholders and HMRC, but file only the balance sheet and notes with Companies House — omitting the profit-and-loss account and directors’ report. Since the overwhelming majority of UK companies are small or micro, the public dataset is, in practice, a balance-sheet dataset.

What it means for you. For most companies there is no turnover and no profit in the public data — not because the extraction missed it, but because it was never filed. One useful quirk: the average-employees note is generally treated as not a profit-and-loss note, so it usually survives filleting, which is why headcount is far better covered than any income-statement item. Credit agencies work around the missing turnover by estimating it from balance-sheet items and sector ratios (Creditsafe publicly describes an “Estimated Turnover Indicator”) — these are modelled figures, not filed ones, so treat them with care.

How it is tagged: iXBRL

iXBRL (“inline XBRL”) is an ordinary-looking HTML accounts document in which each figure is tagged with a machine-readable name. Concretely, a line that displays as “Equity 12,968” is tagged in the HTML as something like:

<ix:nonFraction name="Equity" unitRef="GBP" decimals="0">12,968</ix:nonFraction>

A browser renders “12,968”; a parser reads that the value 12,968 is Equity, in GBP. Each tag also carries a reporting period, a unit (pounds, a count, a ratio), an optional scale (for example, “thousands”), a sign, and optional dimensions (for example, creditors split into “due within one year” and “after one year”).

Tags follow FRC taxonomies — FRS 105 for micro-entities, FRS 102 (including Section 1A) for small and larger companies — which define the machine-readable name for each figure. The taxonomies are revised often, so this project targets each concept’s stable local name and reads across taxonomy generations without a version map. See the accounts guide to pitfalls for what goes wrong at the tag level.

Why the data starts in 2014. iXBRL adoption in the bulk product was gradual, not a hard cutover: roughly 0% of filings were iXBRL in 2010, rising through 2011–2013 and reaching ~97% by 2014. Earlier accounts exist on the server but are barely machine-tagged, so the dataset starts in 2014 — a coverage decision, not a limit of Companies House’s archive. Source: docs/accounts-limitations.md and deck slide 11 notes. [Checked] (project measurement).

What’s changing

When Change
2008 Bulk accounts product begins (e-filed only)
~2014 iXBRL nearly universal in the bulk data
6 April 2025 Size thresholds raised ~50%
2025–26 Identity checks for directors and PSCs (ECCTA)
April 2028 Software-only iXBRL filing; small & micro firms must file a P&L

The 2028 reforms (Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023) will require small companies and micro-entities to file a profit-and-loss account, move all filing to commercial software in iXBRL, and remove the filleted/abridged options. After a pause, this was confirmed on 9 June 2026 for April 2028 (delayed from 2027), and the government has indicated small-company P&L data may be kept private rather than published. Source: ICAEW, June 2026 (deck slide 11 notes; accounts explainer). [Checked] as of June 2026 — the date has already moved once, so re-confirm before relying on it.

What it means for you. Future data will be richer, but 2028 does not fill in history: past filings stay balance-sheet-only, and any newly required small-company P&L may stay private. Anyone modelling on the historical archive must work with balance-sheet-only data for the foreseeable future.
