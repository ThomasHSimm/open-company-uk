The market: who sells company data, and where the value sits

Summary

A market of commercial providers sells UK company data — from credit-reference agencies (Creditsafe, Dun & Bradstreet, Experian, Equifax) through research platforms (Moody’s Bureau van Dijk’s FAME, Beauhurst) to low-cost and freemium tools (Endole, Company Check, OpenCorporates, DataLedger). What they charge for is mostly not the raw data, which is free: it is cleaning, history, linking, a usable interface, scores and support. The important exception is a small private data layer — trade-payment behaviour, bank-credit data and court judgments — that is genuinely not public and carries much of the predictive power behind credit scores. So the fair summary is “most, not all”: open data plus good engineering reproduces most of the descriptive stack, but not the private layer that predicts who will not pay.

All prices below are indicative only, drawn from third-party price guides and vendor pages as summarised in the project deck (2026). They are negotiable, change often, and should be verified before quoting. Each carries a confidence label.

The market splits into three groups: credit-reference agencies (scores, reports, monitoring), research platforms (deep standardised histories and ownership trees, often reached by universities through JISC licences), and low-cost/open tools (repackaged Companies House data, often freemium).

Where the value sits

Think of the stack in four layers, cheapest at the bottom:

Layer What it is Who can do it
Scores, UI, alerts, API, support The product wrapper buyers actually touch Vendors; buyers often pay for reduced risk and effort here
Private data Trade payments, bank-credit (CCDS) data, court judgments Not open; cannot be rebuilt
Cleaning, linking, history, entity IDs Making the raw data usable over time Open pipelines can compete
Open register data Companies House, the Gazette, Land Registry Free

Three conclusions follow, and the deck states them as the honest version rather than the marketing one:

  1. Much of what you pay for is convenience. Filed financials, officers and ownership are free; vendors charge for cleaning, history, linking and a usable interface.
  2. The real advantage is private data. Payment behaviour, bank data and judgment feeds are not public, and they drive the best credit scores. This is why “it’s all open data” holds for most of the stack, not all of it — “most, not all.”
  3. Open data can cover the bottom layers. That is exactly what this project does for the accounts: an open, reconciled, point-in-time-safe version of the descriptive financial layer.

The GIS comparison. In an earlier look at GIS spending in the civil service, most of the underlying geographic data was open and much of the spend went on software licences that made open data easy to use. Company data follows a similar pattern — with one important difference: the private data layer above, which has no equivalent in the GIS case. Source: deck slides 9–10 notes.

What it means for you. For describing companies — who they are, what they filed, who controls them — open data plus good engineering gets you most of the way. For predicting who will not pay, the private layer still matters, and any open model should say so plainly. The sources that make up that private layer, and why they are out of reach, are listed under data you can’t easily get.
