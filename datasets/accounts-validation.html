Accounts dataset: how we know it is right

Summary

A dataset is only as useful as the reasons to trust it. This extractor was checked three ways. First, an accounting invariant: every fact read from a filing must land in exactly one category (kept, duplicate, conflict, skipped…) and the categories must sum to the total, so nothing is lost silently. Second, independent cross-checks: two separate implementations of the restatement calculation produced the same rate, and suspiciously round results flushed out two real bugs that are now covered by regression tests. Third, a parser comparison: the project’s own extractor was run head-to-head against two established tools — the ixbrlparse library and Arelle, a full standards-compliant XBRL processor — on real filings, with Arelle as the tie-breaker where the other two disagreed.

The headline is that on the numbers that actually get published, the project’s extractor agrees with ixbrlparse to 99.999%, while running about twelve times faster — and the comparison itself found and fixed three real bugs in the project’s own parser along the way.

1. The accounting invariant

Every tagged fact in a filing is assigned to exactly one disposition — kept, duplicate, conflict, skipped, and so on — and the per-category counts must add back up to the total facts read. If they don’t, the run fails rather than quietly dropping data. This is the project’s main defence against the failure mode it treats as the enemy: wrong-but-not-crashed. Source: deck slide 13 notes (docs/source-material/companies-house-accounts-deck.md).

2. Independent cross-checks

  • Two implementations, one answer. The restatement rate was computed by two separate engines (a Polars running-tally and an out-of-core DuckDB engine). On the 2022–2023 window they agreed at 7.93% vs 7.94%, matching an independent panel check — evidence the calculation is not an artefact of one implementation. Source: docs/accounts-restatement-2022-2023.md, deck slide 14.
  • Round numbers as a smoke alarm. Suspiciously round results (an exact 0% or 100%) exposed two real bugs during development; both are now covered by regression tests. Source: deck slide 13 notes.
  • Reconciliation and privacy guards. A full-corpus creditors reconciliation found zero sign-flip errors, and an automated staging guard re-scans every published Parquet against the personal-data lists before upload. Source: docs/accounts-creditors-maturity-reconciliation.md, docs/accounts-kaggle-safety-checks.md.

3. The parser comparison

Three parsers were run on the same fixed samples of real Companies House filings, under controlled timing (identical input, warm-up excluded, 3 repeats, median reported), with Arelle as the independent tie-breaker where ours and ixbrlparse disagreed. Source: docs/accounts-validation-summary.md, docs/accounts-parser-check.md.

Speed (5,000-filing fixed sample, end-to-end, 1 worker):

Parser ms/filing Relative to ours
Ours 0.65 1×
ixbrlparse 7.7 ~12× slower
Arelle ~992 (≈1 s) ~1,520× slower

Projected to the full 35,806,258-filing archive at measured 8-worker throughput: ~2.1 h (ours) vs ~1.4 days (ixbrlparse) vs ~94.6 days (Arelle — not viable at this scale, so it serves as a referee on samples, not a production parser). [Checked] (measured; the full-archive figures are projections from the sample throughput — see docs/source-material/deck-corrections.md).

Accuracy: 99.999% agreement on numeric facts between ours and ixbrlparse (119,812 of 119,813 shared numeric facts; 95% CI [99.995%, 100%]; every year 2014–2026 individually at 99.9–100%). Dashes are excluded from this figure because both other parsers resolve a dash to zero immediately while this pipeline defers that to pivot time — the same published value, a design difference not a disagreement. An unfiltered, all-facts comparison shows 89.5% — most of that gap is a deliberate choice to keep non-numeric text as displayed rather than normalising it (e.g. “3 January 2023” vs “2023-01-03”), which never touches a numeric WIDE cell. Source: docs/accounts-validation-summary.md.

What it means for you. For the numbers WIDE actually publishes, the project’s fast extractor and a general-purpose library are effectively interchangeable in accuracy — the speed difference is what makes a full-archive, full-history build practical at all.

The 205-filing ONS tool check

A separate, reproducible check (the script is docs/source-material/tool-comparison-check.py) ran open-source tools over an ONS-curated sample of 379 real filings — 205 iXBRL and 174 plain XML (hand-picked, not random). It is the basis for the “reading a filing is solved” point:

Tool Result on the ONS sample
ixbrlparse 0.11.2 Parsed 205/205 iXBRL and 174/174 XML; read all 202 dash figures as 0; handled all 363 sign="-" figures correctly; exposed 1,679 dimensional facts; ~33 ms per iXBRL file
ixbrl-parse 0.11.0 Parsed only 101/205 — 89 ValueErrors (unit names without a namespace prefix) and 15 KeyErrors
Arelle 2.45.3 Not value-tested here: it needs the FRC taxonomy files, which this sandbox cannot download; offline it returns concept names without values

(The ~33 ms/file here and the 7.7 ms/file in the speed table above are different samples and machines — both are reported with their context, not blended.) Two findings on this sample show what a parser cannot do for you: 164 concept/context pairs repeat within the filings (none disagree), and 99 of the 107 filings that report Creditors give only its parts, not a total. Source: docs/source-material/companies-house-accounts-notes.md, deck slide 12.

What the comparison found in our own parser

The exercise was not a victory lap — it found and fixed three real bugs:

  • Prefix bug — a hardcoded ix: namespace prefix silently extracted zero facts from any filing using a different binding. 61,085 filings recovered (measured directly; an earlier ~291,802 estimate was traced to a stale scan and corrected).
  • Comma-as-decimal formats — misread by ~100–1,000×; 1,096 facts corrected.
  • A hyphenated format name (num-dot-decimal) — silently nulled 540 facts across 3 months; found by tracing published values that disappeared between v1 and v2.
  • Plain-XML filings — 273,418 gained real facts via a new ixbrlparse adapter, verified against Arelle on 186 filings (100% agreement on shared facts).
  • Zero regressions; restatement rate unchanged at 9.36%; fill rates unchanged to within 0.3 percentage points.

Deferred by reasoned decision (documented, not hidden): nested facts (9.85% of filings but only 0.0015% of numeric facts and zero published columns) and text continuations (non-numeric only). Out of scope by design: multi-member and typed-member dimensional contexts (25.3% and 20.1% of filings, 37.9% combined). Source: docs/accounts-validation-summary.md, docs/accounts-limitations.md.

The honest framing. Good open-source parsers already exist; ixbrlparse does single-filing parsing well. What this project adds is the layer around it — full-history bulk orchestration, deduplication, totals-vs-parts rules, cross-filing restatement handling, point-in-time provenance, and a published table. The parsing was never the hard part; deciding which number is true, and when it was known, is. See the dataset and pitfalls.
